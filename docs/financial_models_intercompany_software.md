# Financial Models for Inter-Company Software Transactions

*Optimising accounting efficiency when related companies develop and transact software products*

---

## Context

This guide addresses scenarios where common ownership exists across two companies—one being a software development company that creates products for the other company (the first/primary user). The goal is to structure transactions to maximise asset recognition and minimise expense recognition across both entities when viewed together.

---

## 1. Development Services Agreement (Cost-Plus Model)

### Model Explanation

The software company provides development services to the buyer company under a services agreement. The buyer company pays for development as it occurs, typically at cost plus a margin. The buyer owns the resulting IP from inception.

**Cash flow:** Buyer pays Developer periodically during development  
**IP ownership:** Buyer owns from inception  
**Risk allocation:** Buyer bears development risk

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Developer (Seller) — IFRS 15 / ASC 606:**
- Revenue recognised over time as services rendered
- No intangible asset recognised (IP vests with buyer)
- Costs expensed as incurred or matched to revenue

**Buyer — IAS 38 / GRAP 31 / ASC 350:**
- Costs capitalised as intangible asset once six development criteria met:
  1. Technical feasibility demonstrated
  2. Intention to complete and use/sell
  3. Ability to use or sell the software
  4. Probable future economic benefits
  5. Adequate resources available
  6. Reliable cost measurement
- Prior to criteria being met, costs expensed
- Amortisation begins when software available for use

### Best Fit For

- Large custom development projects where buyer wants full IP control
- Situations where buyer has stronger balance sheet or asset capitalisation needs
- Projects where buyer will commercially exploit the software broadly
- When buyer qualifies for R&D tax incentives (South Africa Section 11D: 150% deduction)

### Compliance Watch Points

- Transfer pricing: Cost-plus margin must be arm's length (typically 5-15% for development services)
- Documentation of when development criteria are met must be contemporaneous
- Buyer must demonstrate control of the asset (not just access to services)
- Research phase costs cannot be capitalised—ensure clear phase demarcation
- Time records and cost allocation methodology must be robust

### Model 1 Variants

**1A. Pure Cost Reimbursement (No Margin)**
- Developer recovers costs only, no profit margin
- Used when maximising buyer's capitalised asset value is priority
- Transfer pricing risk: may not be arm's length unless developer has no comparable third-party work
- Buyer capitalises 100% of reimbursed costs (subject to criteria)

**1B. Cost-Plus Fixed Margin**
- Standard approach: cost plus 5-15% margin
- Margin represents developer's profit, taxable in developer's hands
- Buyer capitalises full amount paid (cost + margin)
- Most defensible for transfer pricing purposes

**1C. Cost-Plus with Performance Bonus**
- Base cost-plus structure with milestone or delivery bonuses
- Bonuses capitalised by buyer if directly attributable to asset
- Developer recognises bonus revenue when performance conditions met
- Variable consideration rules under IFRS 15 apply

**1D. Fixed Price Development Contract**
- Developer bears cost overrun risk
- Buyer has cost certainty, capitalises fixed price
- Developer recognises revenue over time (percentage of completion) or at point in time
- Developer may recognise loss if costs exceed fixed price
- Higher margin typically required to compensate for risk

**1E. Time and Materials**
- Developer bills actual hours at agreed rates plus materials
- Buyer capitalises all payments (subject to criteria)
- No cost overrun risk for developer
- Requires robust time tracking and approval processes
- Rates must be benchmarked for transfer pricing

**1F. Dedicated Development Team (Staff Augmentation)**
- Developer provides personnel who work under buyer's direction
- Closer to secondment than traditional development services
- Buyer has more control—strengthens asset recognition position
- Developer recognises service revenue
- Employment law considerations: who is the employer?

## 2. Software Licence with Royalties

### Model Explanation

The software company develops and owns the IP, then grants a licence to the buyer company. The buyer pays either upfront fees, ongoing royalties, or both. The developer retains ownership and can license to others.

**Cash flow:** Upfront fee and/or usage-based royalties  
**IP ownership:** Developer retains  
**Risk allocation:** Developer bears development risk; buyer bears implementation risk

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Developer (Seller) — IAS 38 + IFRS 15:**
- Development costs capitalised if six criteria met
- Intangible asset recognised on balance sheet
- Amortisation over useful life (typically 3-7 years for software)
- Licence revenue recognised per IFRS 15:
  - Right to use (point in time): recognise upfront
  - Right to access (over time): recognise over licence term
- Royalty revenue recognised as usage occurs

**Buyer — IAS 38 / GRAP 31:**
- Acquired licence capitalised at purchase price plus directly attributable costs
- Implementation and configuration costs: capitalise if they bring asset to working condition
- Training costs: expense
- Ongoing royalties: expense as incurred
- Amortisation over shorter of licence term or useful life

### Best Fit For

- Standard software products with multiple potential customers
- When developer wants to retain IP for future licensing opportunities
- Recurring revenue models preferred by developer
- Buyer prefers OPEX treatment (royalties expensed) rather than large CAPEX

### Compliance Watch Points

- Licence terms must be clearly documented—perpetual vs term, exclusive vs non-exclusive
- Royalty rates must be arm's length (benchmark against third-party software licences)
- Separate performance obligations: licence vs implementation vs support vs updates
- Variable consideration (royalties) requires constraint assessment under IFRS 15
- Related party disclosure required in both sets of financial statements

### Model 2 Variants

**2A. Perpetual Licence (Upfront Payment)**
- Buyer pays once, uses indefinitely
- Developer: revenue recognised at point in time (right to use)
- Buyer: capitalises full amount, amortises over useful life
- No ongoing royalty—clean accounting but developer loses recurring revenue

**2B. Term Licence (Annual/Multi-Year)**
- Buyer pays for defined period, must renew
- Developer: revenue recognised over licence term
- Buyer: capitalises if licence term sufficient to justify; otherwise expense
- Renewal accounting: new asset or continuation depends on terms

**2C. Usage-Based Royalties**
- Buyer pays per transaction, user, or other metric
- Developer: revenue recognised as usage occurs (variable consideration)
- Buyer: expense as incurred, no asset recognition for royalties
- Requires reliable usage tracking and reporting

**2D. Minimum Guarantee Plus Royalties**
- Buyer commits to minimum payment, plus royalties above threshold
- Developer: recognise minimum upfront or over period; excess as earned
- Buyer: capitalise minimum (if qualifies), expense excess royalties
- Hybrid of certainty and upside participation

**2E. Revenue Share / Profit Share**
- Buyer pays percentage of revenue/profit generated using software
- Developer: revenue recognised as buyer earns revenue
- Buyer: expense as cost of sales or operating expense
- Aligns incentives but complex tracking required

**2F. White-Label / Reseller Licence**
- Buyer rebrands and sells to end customers
- Developer retains IP, grants distribution rights
- Developer: royalty revenue as buyer makes sales; or upfront territory fee
- Buyer: may capitalise upfront fees as distribution rights
- End customer accounting separate from intercompany arrangement

**2G. Exclusive vs Non-Exclusive Licence**
- Exclusive: buyer has sole rights (territory, market, or global)
  - Higher upfront fee justified
  - Developer forgoes other licensing opportunities
  - Buyer's asset value higher due to exclusivity
- Non-exclusive: developer can license to others
  - Lower fees but developer retains optionality
  - Buyer's asset may have lower fair value

**2H. Source Code Licence / Escrow**
- Buyer receives or has escrow access to source code
- Stronger control argument—may affect asset recognition
- Developer: consider whether this is licence or sale of IP
- Additional fees typically charged for source code access

## 3. Joint Development / Cost-Sharing Arrangement

### Model Explanation

Both companies contribute resources (cash, personnel, facilities) to jointly develop software. Each party owns rights proportional to their contribution or as contractually agreed. Neither party "sells" to the other.

**Cash flow:** Each party funds their share of costs  
**IP ownership:** Shared/proportional  
**Risk allocation:** Shared proportionally

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Both Parties — IAS 38 / GRAP 31:**
- Each party capitalises their share of development costs when criteria met
- No intercompany revenue or expense for the shared development
- Each party's intangible asset = their contributed costs meeting criteria
- Subsequent exploitation: each party recognises their own revenue from their rights

**If structured as joint arrangement under IFRS 11:**
- Joint operation: recognise assets, liabilities, revenues, expenses for own share
- Joint venture: equity account for interest in joint venture entity

### Best Fit For

- Projects where both companies will use/exploit the resulting software
- Balancing asset recognition across both balance sheets
- When both parties bring complementary capabilities (e.g., one has developers, other has domain expertise)
- Avoiding intercompany profit that gets eliminated on consolidation

### Compliance Watch Points

- Cost-sharing agreement must be documented before development begins
- Contribution allocation must reflect anticipated benefits (arm's length)
- Changes to contribution ratios require prospective adjustment
- If one party contributes more than their benefit share, the excess is a payment to the other party
- Transfer pricing documentation essential—tax authorities scrutinise cost-sharing arrangements
- Exit provisions: what happens if arrangement terminates or one party wants to license to third parties?

### Model 3 Variants

**3A. Proportional Cost Sharing (Equal Ownership)**
- 50/50 cost split and ownership
- Each party capitalises their 50% of qualifying costs
- Simplest structure but may not reflect actual benefit/contribution

**3B. Contribution-Based Sharing**
- Ownership proportional to contribution value
- Cash, personnel, facilities, existing IP all valued
- Requires upfront valuation of non-cash contributions
- More complex but better reflects economic substance

**3C. Benefit-Based Sharing**
- Costs allocated based on anticipated benefits
- Party expecting 70% of benefit bears 70% of costs
- Requires robust benefit projections
- Transfer pricing preferred method (commensurate with income)

**3D. Platform + Application Split**
- One party develops core platform, other develops applications
- Each owns their layer, cross-licences to the other
- Reduces shared ownership complexity
- Clear delineation of IP boundaries required

**3E. Development + Commercialisation Split**
- Developer contributes development capability
- Buyer contributes market access, customer relationships
- Can value contributions differently than cash
- Ongoing royalty or profit split for commercialisation

**3F. Joint Venture Entity**
- Separate legal entity created to hold IP
- Both parties contribute capital/resources to JV
- JV capitalises development costs
- Parents equity account for JV investment
- Clean legal separation but additional entity overhead

**3G. Consortium / Multi-Party Arrangement**
- More than two related parties contribute
- Complex allocation of costs and ownership
- Governance structure critical
- Common in large platform developments

**3H. Pre-Competitive Joint Development**
- Parties develop base technology together
- Each party independently develops competitive applications on top
- Shared base, proprietary extensions
- Common in industry standards development

## 4. Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) Model

### Model Explanation

The developer builds the software, operates/maintains it for a period (often providing SaaS-style access to the buyer), then transfers ownership to the buyer at a predetermined point or price.

**Cash flow:** Service fees during operation period; transfer payment at end  
**IP ownership:** Developer initially; transfers to buyer later  
**Risk allocation:** Developer bears initial risk; transfers with ownership

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Developer — during operation period:**
- Development costs capitalised as intangible asset
- Service revenue recognised over operation period (IFRS 15)
- Asset amortised over expected operation period

**Developer — at transfer:**
- Derecognise intangible asset
- Recognise gain/loss = transfer proceeds minus carrying amount
- Gain not classified as revenue

**Buyer — during operation period:**
- Service fees expensed as incurred
- No asset recognised (SaaS treatment—no control of underlying software)

**Buyer — at transfer:**
- Recognise intangible asset at fair value / acquisition cost
- Begin amortisation from transfer date

### Best Fit For

- Buyer wants to defer capital expenditure
- Developer needs to demonstrate product viability before sale
- Buyer uncertain about long-term needs and wants option to acquire
- Projects where operational data will inform transfer pricing

### Compliance Watch Points

- Transfer price must be determined on arm's length basis
- If transfer is predetermined and price is formula-based, assess whether arrangement is in substance a financing
- Control assessment critical: buyer must not have control during operation period, or accounting changes
- Impairment testing required on developer's intangible asset during operation period
- Document business rationale—tax authorities may challenge delayed transfer structures

### Model 4 Variants

**4A. Fixed Transfer Price**
- Transfer price agreed upfront
- Developer knows exit value, can plan amortisation
- Buyer knows acquisition cost for budgeting
- Risk: market conditions may change, making price off-market at transfer

**4B. Formula-Based Transfer Price**
- Price determined by formula at transfer (e.g., multiple of revenue, cost-plus)
- Adjusts for actual performance during operation
- More arm's length but less certainty
- May be treated as financing if formula ensures recovery of costs plus return

**4C. Fair Market Value at Transfer**
- Independent valuation at transfer date
- Most arm's length approach
- Uncertainty for both parties
- Valuation costs incurred

**4D. Build-Operate-Transfer with Purchase Option**
- Buyer has option (not obligation) to acquire
- Developer bears risk that buyer may not exercise
- Option premium may be charged
- Option accounting applies if premium material

**4E. Build-Operate-Own (BOO)**
- No transfer—developer operates indefinitely
- Essentially becomes SaaS/subscription model
- Included for comparison: developer retains asset permanently
- Buyer never recognises asset

**4F. Build-Transfer-Operate (BTO)**
- Developer builds, transfers immediately, then operates under service contract
- Buyer capitalises asset from transfer date
- Developer provides managed services only
- Buyer has asset on balance sheet sooner

**4G. Build-Lease-Transfer**
- Developer builds, leases to buyer during operation period
- Buyer may recognise right-of-use asset under IFRS 16 (if lease criteria met)
- At end of lease, ownership transfers
- Complex: software leases have specific considerations

**4H. Phased Transfer**
- Modules or components transfer progressively
- Buyer capitalises each component as received
- Developer derecognises progressively
- Suits modular development approaches

## 5. Software Sale with Ongoing Support

### Model Explanation

Developer creates software, sells it outright to buyer, and provides ongoing maintenance/support under a separate service agreement.

**Cash flow:** Upfront purchase price + recurring support fees  
**IP ownership:** Transfers to buyer on sale  
**Risk allocation:** Development risk with developer; ongoing operational risk with buyer

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Developer — IFRS 15 + IAS 38:**
- Development costs capitalised during development
- On sale: derecognise intangible asset, recognise gain/loss
- Support revenue recognised over service period
- If software sale and support are bundled, allocate transaction price to each performance obligation based on standalone selling prices

**Buyer — IAS 38:**
- Recognise software as intangible asset at purchase price
- Allocate portion of price to prepaid support if bundled
- Amortise software over useful life
- Support fees: expense as services received

### Best Fit For

- Clean exit for developer from specific product
- Buyer wants full ownership and control for strategic reasons
- One-time capital injection needed by developer
- Buyer has capability to self-support long-term

### Compliance Watch Points

- Sale price must be arm's length—obtain valuation if material
- Unbundling of sale vs support: use relative standalone selling price method
- If developer has ongoing involvement that gives them control, may not qualify as sale
- Warranties vs support: warranty provisions are liabilities, not deferred revenue
- CGT implications for developer if software not trading stock

### Model 5 Variants

**5A. Clean Sale (No Ongoing Relationship)**
- Outright sale with no post-sale obligations
- Developer completely exits
- Buyer self-supports or engages third party
- Simplest accounting but buyer bears all ongoing risk

**5B. Sale Plus Maintenance Agreement**
- Software sold outright
- Separate maintenance contract for bug fixes, patches
- Maintenance revenue recognised over service period
- Buyer: asset for software, expense for maintenance

**5C. Sale Plus Support and Updates**
- Software sold plus ongoing enhancements included
- Multiple performance obligations under IFRS 15
- Allocate transaction price between software and future updates
- Updates may be capitalised by buyer if they enhance the asset

**5D. Sale with Warranty**
- Software sold with warranty period (typically 90 days - 1 year)
- Warranty is assurance-type: developer accrues warranty provision
- Not a separate performance obligation
- Buyer capitalises full purchase price

**5E. Sale with Buyback Commitment**
- Developer commits to repurchase under certain conditions
- May disqualify as sale if buyback is certain
- Assess whether control has truly transferred
- Could be treated as financing arrangement

**5F. Sale with Retained Improvements**
- Buyer acquires current version
- Developer retains rights to future development/improvements
- Future versions licensed separately
- Clear version demarcation required

**5G. Asset Sale vs Share Sale**
- Asset sale: buyer acquires software IP directly
  - Buyer gets cost base equal to purchase price
  - Developer may have CGT or revenue inclusion
- Share sale: buyer acquires shares in entity holding IP
  - Tax treatment differs significantly
  - Step-up in base cost may or may not occur
  - Consider securities transfer tax (South Africa: 0.25%)

**5H. Sale with Licence-Back**
- Developer sells IP to buyer
- Buyer grants licence back to developer
- Can be used for financing or tax structuring
- Scrutinise economic substance—may be challenged

## 6. Subscription/SaaS Model (No Asset Transfer)

### Model Explanation

Developer hosts and maintains software, providing access to buyer via subscription. No IP ownership transfers. This may be least favourable for combined asset maximisation but included for completeness.

**Cash flow:** Recurring subscription fees  
**IP ownership:** Developer retains  
**Risk allocation:** Developer bears all software risk; buyer bears business dependency risk

### Applicable Accounting Principles

**Developer — IAS 38 + IFRS 15:**
- Development costs capitalised as intangible asset
- Subscription revenue recognised over access period
- Asset amortised over useful life
- Ongoing enhancement costs: expense unless meet capitalisation criteria

**Buyer — no IAS 38 impact:**
- Subscription fees expensed as incurred
- Prepayments recognised as prepaid assets
- Configuration/customisation costs:
  - Expense if developer controls resulting customisation
  - Capitalise only if buyer controls a separable asset

### Best Fit For

- When developer wants recurring revenue and asset on balance sheet
- Buyer prefers operational expenditure (OPEX) for budgeting
- Developer wants to serve multiple customers with same platform
- Not optimal for combined asset maximisation across related entities

### Compliance Watch Points

- Subscription pricing must be arm's length
- Buyer cannot capitalise configuration costs unless they result in a separate asset buyer controls
- Multi-year prepayments: developer recognises deferred revenue liability
- If contract gives buyer right to take possession of software, reassess control and consider as licence

### Model 6 Variants

**6A. Pure SaaS (Multi-Tenant)**
- Buyer accesses shared platform with other customers
- No customisation or dedicated resources
- Developer: single asset serves multiple customers
- Buyer: pure operating expense

**6B. Dedicated Instance (Single-Tenant)**
- Buyer has dedicated environment
- May have more customisation options
- Developer may need to track costs per instance
- Still no asset for buyer unless control transfers

**6C. Subscription with Customisation**
- Base subscription plus custom development
- Customisation accounting depends on who controls the result:
  - Developer controls: buyer expenses customisation fees
  - Buyer controls separate asset: buyer may capitalise
- Key question: can buyer take the customisation elsewhere?

**6D. Hybrid: Subscription + On-Premise Option**
- Buyer can choose cloud or on-premise deployment
- On-premise may trigger licence accounting (right to use)
- Complex: different accounting depending on deployment choice
- Contract terms critical

**6E. Freemium / Tiered Pricing**
- Base functionality free or low cost
- Premium features at higher price
- Developer: recognise revenue for paid tiers
- Buyer: expense subscription fees

**6F. Consumption-Based Pricing**
- Pay per API call, transaction, storage, etc.
- No minimum commitment
- Developer: revenue as consumption occurs
- Buyer: variable operating expense

**6G. Enterprise Agreement (Committed Spend)**
- Buyer commits to minimum annual spend
- May include multiple products/services
- Developer: recognise minimum over period; excess as earned
- Buyer: expense over period; prepayments as prepaid assets

**6H. Private Label SaaS**
- Developer operates platform
- Buyer rebrands for their customers
- Developer: service revenue from buyer
- Buyer: revenue from end customers, cost of sales to developer
- No IP transfer—developer retains and operates

**6I. Managed Service with Transition Rights**
- SaaS with contractual right to insource later
- If buyer exercises, accounting transitions to licence or sale model
- Option value may affect pricing
- Document transition mechanics clearly

## Comparative Summary

| Model | Developer Asset | Buyer Asset | Combined Asset Position | Arm's Length Risk |
|-------|----------------|-------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Development Services (Cost-Plus) | None | High (capitalises costs) | Buyer-weighted | Margin benchmarking |
| Licence with Royalties | High | Medium (licence cost) | Developer-weighted | Royalty rate benchmarking |
| Joint Development | Proportional | Proportional | Balanced | Contribution allocation |
| Build-Operate-Transfer | High initially, then none | None initially, then high | Shifts over time | Transfer price |
| Software Sale + Support | None after sale | High | Buyer-weighted | Sale price valuation |
| SaaS/Subscription | High | None | Developer-weighted | Subscription pricing |

### Model Selection by Capitalisation Priority

When selecting a model based on where the asset should appear on balance sheets:

| Capitalisation Priority | Recommended Models | Rationale |
|------------------------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Buyer balance sheet** | Model 1 (Cost-Plus), Model 5 (Sale) | Buyer capitalises development costs or purchase price; Developer has no ongoing asset |
| **Developer balance sheet** | Model 2 (Licence), Model 6 (SaaS) | Developer retains IP and capitalises development; Buyer expenses fees or capitalises licence only |
| **Both balance sheets** | Model 3 (Joint Development), Model 4 (BOT) | Model 3: Both parties capitalise proportional shares; Model 4: Developer capitalises initially, then transfers to Buyer |
| **Minimise assets** | Model 6 (SaaS) | Buyer expenses subscription fees; Developer's asset is the only one recognised |
| **Tax efficiency** | Model 1 or Model 5 | Buyer can claim Section 11(e) accelerated depreciation on capitalised software |

## South African Tax Considerations

### Section 11D R&D Incentive
- 150% deduction on qualifying operational R&D expenditure
- Available until 31 December 2033
- Activities must be approved by Minister of Higher Education, Science and Innovation
- Both entities could potentially qualify if both undertake qualifying R&D

### Section 11(e) Wear and Tear
- Self-developed mainframe software: 5 years write-off
- PC software: 2 years write-off
- Applies to entity that owns the software

### General Principle
- Amortisation of intangibles generally not tax deductible
- Structure to maximise allocation to tangible assets where possible
- Acquired patents: Section 11(gC) allows 5% per annum deduction

### Transfer Pricing
- Related party transactions must be at arm's length prices
- SARS actively scrutinises intercompany software arrangements
- Maintain contemporaneous documentation including:
  - Functional analysis
  - Comparability analysis
  - Selection of transfer pricing method
  - Economic analysis supporting pricing

## Key Compliance Checklist (All Models)

- [ ] Written agreement in place before transaction commences
- [ ] Transfer pricing analysis and documentation prepared
- [ ] Development phase vs research phase clearly documented with dates
- [ ] Cost tracking systems adequate to support capitalisation
- [ ] Control assessment documented (who controls the asset?)
- [ ] Useful life determination documented with supporting rationale
- [ ] Related party disclosures prepared for both entities
- [ ] Impairment indicators assessed at each reporting date
- [ ] Deferred tax implications calculated (accounting vs tax treatment differences)
- [ ] Consolidation elimination entries identified if consolidated financial statements prepared
