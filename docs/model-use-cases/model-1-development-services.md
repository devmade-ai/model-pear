# Model 1: Development Services (Cost-Plus) — Use Cases

> **Purpose**: When to use each variant of the Development Services model
> **Variants**: 6 (1A–1F)
> **Core Concept**: Developer provides custom development services; Buyer controls IP from inception

---

## Overview

Model 1 is best when the **Buyer wants to own the IP outright** from the start. The Developer acts as a contractor/service provider, and the Buyer capitalises the development as an intangible asset.

**Choose Model 1 over other models when:**
- Buyer needs full IP ownership and control
- Custom software built to Buyer's specifications
- Developer is essentially a contractor/development agency
- Payment tied to delivery progress (not lump-sum purchase)

---

## Variant 1A: Pure Cost Reimbursement

### Description
Developer recovers costs only — zero markup margin.

### Best Scenario
**Consolidated entities** where intercompany margin would be eliminated at group level anyway.

### Real-World Example
> A holding company's IT subsidiary develops an internal ERP system for the parent company. Since both entities consolidate, any markup would be eliminated in group accounts. Pure cost reimbursement simplifies accounting and avoids creating artificial intercompany profit.

### Why 1A Over Other Variants
- Avoids redundant profit layers in consolidated groups
- Useful for cost validation when Developer lacks third-party benchmarks
- Maximises Buyer's asset base (full spend capitalises without margin leakage)

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Limited TP risk since no profit element exists. However, must ensure costs are arm's length if entities are related but don't consolidate.

---

## Variant 1B: Cost-Plus Fixed Margin

### Description
Standard cost-plus with fixed percentage markup (typically 5–15%).

### Best Scenario
**Standard arm's length arrangement** between independent parties where transfer pricing defensibility matters.

### Real-World Example
> A software development agency builds a custom inventory management system for a retail client. The contract specifies development costs plus 12% markup. This margin is within OECD guidelines for development services (5–15%), making it defensible to tax authorities.

### Why 1B Over Other Variants
- Most defensible TP position (well-established benchmark range)
- Simple to calculate and document
- Fair compensation without complex incentive structures
- Standard industry practice for development services

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
5–15% markup is the safest range. Document comparable margins from similar transactions.

---

## Variant 1C: Cost-Plus with Performance Bonus

### Description
Base margin (e.g., 10%) plus milestone-based bonuses for quality or timing achievements.

### Best Scenario
**Projects where Developer's performance directly impacts Buyer's success** — on-time delivery, quality thresholds, or milestone achievement matter.

### Real-World Example
> A fintech company contracts a Developer to build a payment gateway before a regulatory deadline. Contract: cost + 8% base margin + 5% bonus if delivered 2 weeks early + 3% bonus for zero critical defects at launch. Developer earns up to 16% if all bonuses achieved.

### Why 1C Over Other Variants
- Aligns Developer's incentives with Buyer's priorities
- Creates skin in the game for quality and timing
- Allows Developer to earn higher margin through performance
- Buyer pays premium only if value delivered

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Document bonus criteria objectively. Expected bonus should be probability-weighted for revenue recognition.

---

## Variant 1D: Fixed Price Development Contract

### Description
Lump sum payment regardless of actual costs — Developer bears cost overrun risk.

### Best Scenario
**Well-defined scope** where Developer is confident in cost estimates and Buyer demands budget certainty.

### Real-World Example
> A government department procures a document management system with detailed RFP specifications. Fixed price of R5M agreed. If Developer spends R4M, margin is 25%. If costs reach R5.5M, Developer absorbs the loss. Buyer gets budget certainty; Developer accepts risk for potential upside.

### Why 1D Over Other Variants
- Buyer gets complete budget certainty
- Developer can earn higher margin if efficient
- Forces thorough scoping and estimation discipline
- Common in competitive tender environments

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Margin is unknown until project completes. Document that fixed pricing reflects scope certainty and Developer's risk assumption.

---

## Variant 1E: Time and Materials

### Description
Hourly/daily rates plus material costs — no fixed scope or total price.

### Best Scenario
**Evolving or uncertain scope** where requirements will change (agile development, rapid prototyping, R&D projects).

### Real-World Example
> A startup engages a Developer for a 6-month discovery and MVP phase. Requirements are unclear and will evolve based on user feedback. Contract: Senior developers at R1,800/hour, juniors at R900/hour, plus cloud infrastructure costs. Weekly time reports approved by Buyer.

### Why 1E Over Other Variants
- Accommodates scope changes without renegotiation
- Fair when requirements genuinely uncertain
- Avoids scope creep penalties for Developer
- Hourly rates benchmarkable against market

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Hourly rates are defensible if comparable to market rates for equivalent skill levels. Document rate benchmarking.

---

## Variant 1F: Dedicated Development Team

### Description
Monthly retainer for dedicated resources — staff augmentation model with Buyer directing the work.

### Best Scenario
**Long-term engagement (12+ months)** where Buyer wants control over Developer personnel and work direction.

### Real-World Example
> A bank engages a Developer to provide a dedicated 8-person team for ongoing digital transformation. Monthly retainer of R1.2M covers salaries, management overhead, and margin. Team works exclusively on bank projects, reports to bank's IT director, and follows bank's processes.

### Why 1F Over Other Variants
- Predictable monthly costs for budgeting
- Buyer controls work priorities and direction
- Closer integration than project-based engagement
- Resource commitment certainty (FTEs reserved)

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Retainer should reflect market rates for equivalent team composition. Buyer's control strengthens argument that resulting IP is Buyer's property.

---

## Variant Selection Guide

| Scenario | Best Variant |
|----------|--------------|
| Consolidated entities | **1A** — no margin needed |
| Independent parties, standard engagement | **1B** — defensible TP |
| Quality/timing critical | **1C** — performance incentive |
| Well-defined scope, budget certainty needed | **1D** — fixed price |
| Uncertain/evolving scope | **1E** — time & materials |
| Long-term, integrated engagement | **1F** — dedicated team |

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Using 1D (fixed price) with unclear scope** — leads to disputes or Developer losses
2. **Using 1A (zero margin) for unrelated parties** — may not be arm's length
3. **Using 1E (T&M) without rate benchmarking** — TP vulnerability
4. **Using 1B margin outside 5–15% range** — harder to defend to tax authorities
