# Model 2: Software Licence with Royalties — Use Cases

> **Purpose**: When to use each variant of the Software Licence model
> **Variants**: 8 (2A–2H)
> **Core Concept**: Developer retains IP ownership and grants usage rights to Buyer

---

## Overview

Model 2 is best when the **Developer wants to retain IP and monetise it across multiple licensees**. The Developer keeps the asset on their books and earns recurring revenue; the Buyer gets usage rights without ownership.

**Choose Model 2 over other models when:**
- Developer wants to license the same software to multiple parties
- Developer prefers recurring revenue over one-time sale
- Buyer doesn't need full IP ownership
- Variable pricing aligned with Buyer's usage/success is desired

**Cross-border note:** Royalty payments between jurisdictions may attract withholding tax (commonly 10–15%). This affects net economics and should be factored into pricing. Check applicable tax treaties.

---

## Variant 2A: Perpetual Licence (Upfront Payment)

### Description
One-time payment for indefinite use rights. Developer retains IP; Buyer owns the licence forever.

### Best Scenario
**Developer needs immediate capital** and Buyer wants permanent rights with a simple, final transaction.

### Real-World Example
> A mid-size accounting firm purchases a perpetual licence for tax compliance software at R500,000. They pay once and can use the software indefinitely. The Developer recognises full revenue at grant date and moves on. No ongoing relationship required unless Buyer wants optional support.

### Why 2A Over Other Variants
- Simplest licence structure
- Developer gets cash immediately
- Buyer avoids ongoing licence fees
- Clean transaction with no renewal management
- Revenue recognised at grant date if licence is standalone (note: under IFRS 15, bundled support or updates may require separate performance obligation treatment and recognition over time)

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Upfront fee benchmarkable against comparable perpetual licence transactions. Document market comparables.

---

## Variant 2B: Term Licence (Annual/Multi-Year)

### Description
Fixed period licence (1–5 years) with renewal options. Buyer pays annually or upfront for the term.

### Best Scenario
**Buyer wants flexibility to exit** and Developer wants ongoing relationship touchpoints through renewals.

### Real-World Example
> A logistics company licenses fleet management software for a 3-year term at R150,000/year. At year 3, they can renew, switch vendors, or negotiate new terms. If better technology emerges, they're not locked in forever. Developer gets predictable revenue with renewal opportunities.

### Why 2B Over Other Variants
- Buyer flexibility (no permanent commitment)
- Developer gets recurring touchpoints (renewal conversations)
- Lower upfront cost than perpetual
- Technology refresh option at term end
- Revenue recognised over licence term

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Annual fee benchmarkable against term licence comparables. Multi-year discount should be documented.

---

## Variant 2C: Usage-Based Royalties

### Description
Pay per transaction, user, API call, or other usage metric. Purely variable ongoing payments.

### Best Scenario
**Usage is unpredictable** and Buyer wants costs directly aligned with value received.

### Real-World Example
> A payment processor licenses fraud detection software at R0.02 per transaction screened. In a slow month (1M transactions), they pay R20,000. In peak season (10M transactions), they pay R200,000. Cost scales with business volume — when they earn more, Developer earns more.

### Why 2C Over Other Variants
- Perfect cost-value alignment
- Low commitment for Buyer (no upfront capital)
- Developer participates in Buyer's growth
- Suits high-volume, variable workloads
- Revenue recognised as usage occurs

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Royalty rate benchmarkable (e.g., industry royalty rate studies). Requires robust usage metering.

---

## Variant 2D: Minimum Guarantee Plus Royalties

### Description
Floor payment guarantees Developer baseline revenue, plus variable royalties above threshold.

### Best Scenario
**Developer needs revenue certainty** but Buyer expects high usage and wants upside participation.

### Real-World Example
> A media streaming platform licenses content recommendation engine. Contract: R500,000 minimum annual guarantee + R0.001 per recommendation served above 500M threshold. Developer gets R500k floor even if platform underperforms. If platform serves 2B recommendations, Developer earns R500k + R1.5M = R2M.

### Why 2D Over Other Variants
- Developer gets guaranteed minimum (risk mitigation)
- Buyer gets variable upside if exceeding threshold
- Balances certainty with performance alignment
- Common in content/media licensing
- Suits unpredictable usage where Developer cannot accept zero-revenue risk (unlike pure 2C)

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Minimum should reflect arm's length floor. Royalty rate above minimum benchmarked separately.

---

## Variant 2E: Revenue Share / Profit Share

### Description
Developer receives percentage of Buyer's actual earnings generated using the software.

### Best Scenario
**Software directly generates Buyer's revenue** and both parties want strong alignment on success.

### Real-World Example
> A travel booking platform licenses an AI pricing optimisation engine. Contract: Developer gets 8% of incremental revenue attributable to dynamic pricing (measured via controlled testing comparing optimised vs baseline pricing). If optimisation generates R50M additional bookings, Developer earns R4M. Complete alignment — Developer succeeds only when Buyer succeeds.

### Why 2E Over Other Variants
- Strongest possible incentive alignment
- Developer shares Buyer's commercial risk
- Simple to explain ("you get 8% of what it makes")
- Suits software with measurable revenue impact

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Highest TP complexity of all Model 2 variants. Key challenges: (1) defining the revenue base (gross vs net); (2) attribution of costs in profit share arrangements; (3) verification rights and audit provisions; (4) comparable transactions are rare. Requires transparent Buyer financials, robust attribution methodology, and detailed documentation.

## Variant 2F: White-Label / Reseller Licence

### Description
Buyer rebrands Developer's software and sells to end customers. Developer earns from Buyer's downstream sales.

### Best Scenario
**Developer lacks market distribution capability** and Buyer has established channel to reach end customers.

### Real-World Example
> A niche CRM developer licenses their platform to a large consulting firm. The consulting firm rebrands it as "ConsultCo CRM" and sells to their SME clients. Developer receives R50,000 setup fee + R200 per end-customer seat per year. Developer gets market reach; Buyer gets product to sell.

### Why 2F Over Other Variants
- Developer accesses markets without sales force
- Buyer gets product without development cost
- Two-tier revenue (upfront + ongoing royalties)
- Scales with Buyer's end-customer success

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Reseller margin benchmarkable against comparable distribution arrangements (varies significantly by industry, software type, and channel maturity — SaaS resellers often 15–25%; enterprise software can exceed 40%). Developer royalty separate from Buyer's resale economics.

---

## Variant 2G: Exclusive vs Non-Exclusive Licence

### Description
Exclusive licence grants sole rights in territory/market segment. Commands premium over non-exclusive.

### Best Scenario
**Buyer needs competitive protection** in their market and is willing to pay premium for exclusivity.

### Real-World Example
> A regional bank licenses fraud detection software with exclusive rights for South African retail banking. Non-exclusive licence: R1M. Exclusive licence: R1.8M (80% premium). The bank's competitors cannot license the same software in SA, giving the bank a competitive moat. Developer foregoes other SA banking customers for the premium.

### Why 2G Over Other Variants
- Buyer gets competitive advantage
- Developer earns exclusivity premium (highly fact-specific — depends on market size, competitive landscape, and foregone licensing opportunities)
- Clear market definition limits scope
- Strategic value beyond software functionality

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Exclusivity premium benchmarkable. Document comparable exclusive vs non-exclusive transactions. Note: exclusivity restricts Developer's ability to license elsewhere, which may affect DEMPE analysis — the premium must reflect foregone licensing value.

---

## Variant 2H: Source Code Licence / Escrow

### Description
Buyer gets access to source code (directly or via escrow) as insurance against Developer failure.

### Best Scenario
**Buyer concerned about Developer business continuity** for mission-critical software dependency.

### Real-World Example
> A hospital licenses patient management software. Given criticality, they require source code escrow: code held by third-party escrow agent, released to hospital upon trigger events (Developer bankruptcy, product abandonment, failure to maintain, material breach, or change of control). Hospital pays R2M licence + R50k escrow setup + R10k/year escrow maintenance. Peace of mind for mission-critical system.

### Why 2H Over Other Variants
- Buyer independence from Developer continuity
- Reduces vendor lock-in risk
- Enables self-maintenance if Developer fails
- Common for mission-critical deployments

### Transfer Pricing Consideration
Source code premium benchmarkable (typically 10–30% above binary-only licence). Escrow fees separate.

## Variant Selection Guide

| Scenario | Best Variant |
|----------|--------------|
| Simple transaction, Buyer wants permanent rights | **2A** — perpetual upfront |
| Buyer wants flexibility, Developer wants renewals | **2B** — term licence |
| Variable/unpredictable usage | **2C** — usage-based royalties |
| Developer needs floor, Buyer expects high volume | **2D** — minimum + royalties |
| Software directly drives Buyer's revenue | **2E** — revenue share |
| Buyer will resell to end customers | **2F** — white-label |
| Buyer needs competitive protection | **2G** — exclusive |
| Mission-critical, continuity concerns | **2H** — source code escrow |

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Using 2A (perpetual) when Developer plans ongoing enhancements** — foregoes future monetisation opportunity; consider 2B term licence instead
2. **Using 2E (revenue share) without attribution methodology** — leads to disputes
3. **Granting 2G (exclusivity) without clear market definition** — scope creep
4. **Using 2C (usage-based) without robust metering** — billing disputes
5. **Underpricing 2G exclusivity premium** — leaving value on table
